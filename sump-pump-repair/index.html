Pump repair for the places Surprise homes actually use pumps

A sump pump moves water out of a pit to keep a low space dry. A sewage ejector pump lifts wastewater from fixtures that sit below the sewer line up to where it can drain. Both rely on a float switch, a motor, and a check valve to keep working.

Surprise is not a basement city. Most homes here sit on concrete slabs, so a classic basement sump pump is rare. Pumps still show up, though, in casitas and guest suites added below the main drain line, in the few custom homes with basements or below-grade rooms, in yard and patio drainage pits, and in commercial buildings.

When one of those pumps fails, the result is usually a fixture that will not drain, an alarm that will not stop, or standing water where a pump should have removed it. Repair starts by finding which part quit.

When to call: signs a pump is failing

These are the symptoms people notice first.

  • 1An alarm keeps sounding. High-water alarms mean the pump is not keeping up or has stopped.
  • 2A fixture will not drain. A sink, shower, or toilet served by an ejector backs up when the pump fails.
  • 3The pump runs nonstop. A stuck float or a leaking check valve can keep the motor running.
  • 4Humming without pumping. A jammed impeller or a failed motor can hum without moving water.
  • 5Odors near the basin. Sewage smell can mean a cracked lid seal or a vent problem.

What the plumbing code expects from a pump system

Section 712 of the International Plumbing Code (IPC) covers sumps and ejectors. In plain terms, a sewage pump needs a sealed basin, a vent, and a check valve plus a shutoff valve on the discharge line so wastewater cannot flow back into the basin.

Many pump problems come from parts of the setup rather than the pump itself. A missing check valve, a blocked vent, or an undersized basin can make a good pump fail early.

Repair, replace, or add a pump

The right step depends on the pump's age and what failed.

Fix the failed part

Floats, switches, check valves, and alarm panels can usually be replaced without changing the pump.

This is the right first step for a pump that is only a few years old.

Best when

One part has failed

A quick fix often restores the whole system.

Install a new pump

A burned-out motor or a cracked housing usually means a new pump. It is sized to the basin and the height it has to lift.

Replacing the check valve at the same time is often worth it.

Best when

Motor or housing failure

Matching pump size to the job prevents early failure.

Adding a bathroom or kitchen to a casita below the sewer line usually needs an ejector system.
