Garage Water Heaters in Surprise Summers: What the Heat Does

Most water heaters in Surprise live in the garage, where summer air gets far hotter than the living space. That location changes how they wear, fail, and should be maintained.

By the Surprise Express Plumbing team, September 25, 2026

Walk into most Surprise garages on a July afternoon and you will find the water heater tucked in a corner, sitting in air that can feel like an oven. Builders put it there for good reasons. The garage keeps a gas appliance away from living space, gives easy access for service, and saves floor space inside the house. But the location means your water heater lives a harder life than one in a basement in Ohio.

This guide covers what summer heat actually does to a tank water heater, which problems show up most in hot garages, and what you can check yourself before the next stretch of triple-digit days.

What heat does, and does not, do to a water heater

A hot garage does not make your water heater work harder to heat water. In fact, warmer incoming water and warmer surrounding air mean the burner or elements run less in summer than in winter. That part is good news.

The strain comes from somewhere else. Heat speeds up the aging of rubber, plastic, and electronic parts. Gaskets on the drain valve, the plastic dip tube inside the tank, control boards on newer gas units, and thermostats all sit in that air day after day. Parts that might last many years in a cool space can wear faster in a garage that stays hot for months.

Pressure and the relief valve

Every tank water heater has a temperature and pressure relief valve, usually on the top or upper side, with a pipe running down or out of the garage. It opens to release water if the temperature or pressure inside the tank gets too high. It is one of the most important safety parts in the house.

In summer, people often notice that valve dripping or the discharge pipe leaving a wet spot. Sometimes the cause is simply thermal expansion. When water heats, it expands, and if a check valve or pressure regulator keeps that water from flowing back into the street line, pressure in the house rises until the relief valve lets some out.

An expansion tank solves that problem by giving the expanding water somewhere to go. If your relief valve drips after long hot water use, ask whether your system needs one or whether the existing one has lost its air charge. Never cap or plug a dripping relief valve. It is there to keep the tank from failing violently.

Scale gets worse with heat

Hard water minerals come out of solution faster as water gets hotter. In a tank, they settle to the bottom as sediment. On a gas water heater, that layer sits between the burner and the water, so the burner has to heat through it. The result is the popping or rumbling many people hear, along with longer heating times and extra stress on the tank bottom.

Surprise water ranges from moderately hard to hard depending on the supplier, so sediment is a real factor here. Draining a few gallons from the valve at the bottom once or twice a year helps. If the tank has gone years without a flush, go slowly, because a heavy flush can clog or damage an aged drain valve.

Gas water heaters need air and clearance

Gas water heaters burn fuel, so they need a steady supply of combustion air and a clear, properly sloped vent to carry exhaust outside. In a garage, stored items have a way of piling up around the heater over time. Boxes, paint cans, and gasoline containers should stay well away from it.

Plumbing and fuel gas codes generally require gas appliances with a flame to sit at least 18 inches above the garage floor. Fuel vapors are heavier than air and settle low, so lifting the burner keeps it away from them. If your heater sits on the floor, it was either installed before that rule or needs a closer look.

A pre-summer water heater checklist

  1. Look at the relief valve discharge pipe. It should run to a safe spot and be dry most of the time.
  2. Check the floor under the tank for rust stains or dampness.
  3. Clear stored items at least a few feet away from a gas heater and its vent.
  4. Confirm a gas heater sits on a stand that raises the burner off the floor.
  5. Find the cold water shutoff above the tank and make sure it turns.
  6. Flush a few gallons from the drain valve if the tank has been flushed before.
  7. Read the data label and note the build year.

How to read the build year

Most brands hide the manufacture date inside the serial number on the data label. The format varies by brand, but many start with a letter for the month and two digits for the year, or with the year first. A quick search for your brand plus the phrase serial number date code will usually show you how to read it.

Knowing the age matters. Tank water heaters commonly last around 8 to 12 years, and a hot garage plus hard water tends to push that toward the shorter end. A heater past 10 years that starts leaking from the tank body is rarely worth repairing.

Is a heat pump water heater a good fit for a hot garage?

This is one place where the heat works in your favor. A heat pump water heater pulls warmth from the surrounding air to heat the water. The warmer the air, the more efficiently it runs, and a garage here is warm most months. As a side effect, it also cools and dehumidifies the garage slightly while it runs.

Heat pump units need room around them for airflow, a condensate drain, and usually a dedicated electrical circuit. They cost more up front than a standard electric tank. For households already on electric water heating, they are worth a serious look, and our water heater replacement page compares them with gas and tankless options.

When to call

  • Water pooling under the tank or rust at the base.
  • A relief valve that discharges more than a small drip.
  • No hot water, or hot water that runs out much sooner than it used to.
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit.
  • A smell of gas near the heater, which means leaving the house first and calling from outside.

Many of these are single-part fixes such as a thermocouple, gas valve, or element, and a water heater repair handles them. A leaking tank body is the exception. At that point replacement is the only real answer, and getting the new unit sized, raised off the floor, and fitted with the right expansion protection sets it up for a longer life in the same hot garage.

Rural homes to the northwest often run propane heaters in detached garages and sheds, which is a common call for plumbers in Wittmann.

The takeaway

Heat does not make a garage water heater work harder, but it does age its parts faster and makes scale and pressure problems more likely. A few minutes of checking before summer, a yearly flush, and the right safety parts go a long way. Knowing the age of your heater lets you plan a replacement on your schedule rather than on the tank's.
