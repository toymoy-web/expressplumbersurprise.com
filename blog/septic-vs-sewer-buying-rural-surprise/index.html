Septic vs Sewer When Buying Near Wittmann or Waddell

Homes on larger lots west and north of Surprise often run on a septic tank and sometimes a private well. Here is what that means for a buyer and what to ask before closing.

By the Surprise Express Plumbing team, September 25, 2026

Inside Surprise city limits, most homes are connected to city sewer and draw water from a utility. Head west toward Waddell or north along Grand Avenue toward Wittmann, and the picture changes. Larger lots, horse properties, and older rural homes often rely on a septic system for waste and sometimes a private well for water.

Neither is a problem in itself. Well-maintained septic systems and wells serve many homes for decades. But they come with responsibilities and costs that city homes do not have, and a buyer should understand them before closing.

How to tell if a home is on septic

  • Ask the seller directly and ask for any septic records.
  • Look for a sewer charge on the seller's utility bills. No sewer charge usually means septic.
  • Walk the yard for tank lids or risers, often within a short distance of the house.
  • Check county records. In unincorporated Maricopa County, septic permits are handled by the county's environmental services department.
  • Ask the listing agent whether a septic inspection has been ordered.

Arizona's inspection at sale

According to ADEQ rules, a conventional septic system has to be inspected before a property transfers. A qualified inspector does the work ahead of closing, and the tank is typically pumped so the inspector can see its condition.

That report is one of the most useful documents a rural buyer can get. It tells you the tank's size and condition, whether the baffles and outlet are intact, and whether there are signs of trouble in the drain field. Read it closely and ask questions about anything flagged.

Questions to ask the seller

  1. When was the tank last pumped, and do you have the receipt?
  2. How big is the tank, and where exactly is it and the drain field?
  3. Have there been any backups, alarms, or repairs?
  4. Does the system include a pump or an alarm panel?
  5. Has anything been built or paved over the drain field?
  6. Is the garbage disposal used heavily?

What a septic system needs from you

A septic tank separates solids from liquid. Solids settle to the bottom as sludge, grease floats as scum, and the liquid in the middle flows out to the drain field, where the soil treats it. Over time, sludge and scum build up and have to be pumped out.

A common starting interval for pumping is every three to five years, depending on the tank size and how many people live in the home. Skipping pumpings lets solids escape to the drain field, the costliest piece of the system to replace. Our septic tank pumping page covers intervals and warning signs.

Private wells

Some rural homes also draw water from a private well. Federal drinking water rules cover public water systems. A private well is on its own, so testing and treatment fall to the owner. A buyer should ask for recent water test results and consider a new lab test before closing.

A well system includes the pump in the well, a pressure tank, and the lines to the house. Ask about the pump's age, the well's depth, and any history of low pressure or sediment. Many well owners add filtration based on lab results, which our water filtration page explains.

Long lines and outdoor plumbing

Rural lots often mean long water lines from a meter or well to the house, a barn, or troughs. Those lines can leak underground without showing up for a long time. A buyer should find out where they run and whether any have been repaired.

Outdoor faucets serving livestock need backflow protection, and horse properties often have more hose bibs than a typical home. Check each one for drips and working vacuum breakers.

Propane

Homes beyond natural gas service often run water heaters and ranges on propane from a tank on the property. Ask who supplies the propane, whether the tank is owned or leased, and how old the water heater is. A propane water heater uses many of the same parts as a natural gas unit and is repaired the same way.

What happens during a pump-out

A septic contractor locates and uncovers the tank lid, then pumps out the sludge and scum with a vacuum truck. While the tank is open, a good contractor looks at the inlet and outlet baffles, the condition of the tank walls, and the level of liquid, which can hint at drain field problems if it is higher than it should be.

If the lid is buried, adding a riser that brings the access up to ground level makes every future pump-out faster and cheaper. It is a small upgrade worth considering early in ownership.

Caring for the drain field

The drain field is where most of the treatment happens and where the biggest repair costs hide. Keep vehicles, trailers, and livestock off it, since heavy weight compacts the soil. Do not plant trees nearby, because roots seek out the moisture in the lines. Point roof runoff and monsoon water away from it so the soil is not flooded during storms.

Red flags in an inspection report

  • Liquid levels above the outlet, which can point to a struggling drain field.
  • Damaged or missing baffles.
  • Signs of effluent surfacing in the yard.
  • No pumping record for many years.
  • Structures or paving placed over the drain field.

A buyer's plumbing checklist for rural lots

  1. Confirm septic or sewer.
  2. Get and read the septic transfer inspection report.
  3. Get the pumping history and tank location.
  4. If there is a well, get recent water tests and pump details.
  5. Find out where long water lines run and whether any have leaked.
  6. Check water pressure at several fixtures.
  7. Look at the water heater's age and fuel type.
  8. Test every outdoor faucet and look for backflow devices.

Budgeting for rural systems

Build septic pumping into your yearly budget as a set-aside, even though the bill comes every few years. Add a line for well water testing and filter changes if the home has a well. Setting money aside for these predictable costs makes a rural home far easier to own than waiting for them to arrive as surprises.

It is also worth asking your insurance agent what is covered. Many policies leave out septic tanks, wells, and buried service lines unless extra coverage is bought.

The takeaway

A home on septic and a well can be a great fit for a buyer who wants space and quiet. It simply comes with a different kind of upkeep. Knowing the condition of those systems before you close, and budgeting for their maintenance, keeps rural living from bringing expensive surprises.
