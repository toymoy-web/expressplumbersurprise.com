Sun City Grand Homes at 20 Years and Beyond: Which Plumbing Parts Are Due

Sun City Grand opened in 1996, and its final homes went up about a decade later. At 20 to 30 years old, many of its original plumbing parts are reaching the end of their normal life.

By the Surprise Express Plumbing team, September 25, 2026

Sun City Grand is one of the best-known communities in Surprise. Del Webb opened it in 1996 as an active adult community, and building continued until about 2006. That means nearly every home there now has plumbing that is somewhere between 20 and 30 years old.

That is not old for a house. It is, however, the age when many of the smaller parts inside the plumbing system reach the end of their expected life all at once. Knowing which ones lets you replace them on your own schedule instead of after a leak.

Why 20 to 30 years is a turning point

Pipes in a home this age are usually fine for years to come. The parts that wear out are the ones that move, seal, or flex: rubber washers, plastic fill valves, braided supply lines, and valves that sit untouched for decades. Hard water and a hot garage speed that wear along.

In an active adult community, there is another factor. Many homes have been lived in by the same owners for years, often with long stretches of summer vacancy. A part that fails in an empty house can cause far more damage than the same failure with someone home.

Kitchen

  • Angle stop valves under the sink. Original multi-turn valves often seize or drip when finally turned.
  • Faucet supply lines. Older plastic or early braided lines can bulge or crack at the nut.
  • The dishwasher supply and drain connections, which sit hidden behind the appliance.
  • The refrigerator ice maker line, a thin tube that is easy to kink and slow to show a leak.
  • The garbage disposal, which commonly lasts around 10 years.

Bathrooms

  • Toilet fill valves and flappers, which wear out and leak water into the bowl silently.
  • Toilet supply lines and the shutoff valve behind each toilet.
  • Wax rings under toilets that have rocked or been reset over the years.
  • Shower valve cartridges that stiffen or drip after decades of hard water.
  • Tub spouts and diverters that no longer send all the water to the showerhead.

Garage and laundry

  • The water heater. Few tanks last 20 years, so most homes are on their second or third, and the current one may be due.
  • Washing machine hoses. Rubber hoses are a well-known cause of floods and are worth swapping for braided stainless.
  • The softener, if the home has one. Resin and control valves wear out over time.
  • The pressure reducing valve, if one is installed. A failing valve lets high street pressure into the home.

Outside

  • Hose bibs, which take years of sun and heavy use.
  • Irrigation valves and drip lines, which crack in the heat.
  • The backflow device on the irrigation line, if the home has one.
  • The main shutoff valve, which may not have been turned in years.

The part that causes the most damage

If you replace nothing else, look hard at supply lines, the short flexible hoses that connect toilets, faucets, and appliances to the shutoff valves. They sit under full water pressure around the clock. When one fails, it releases water at full flow until someone closes a valve.

In an occupied home, that is a messy afternoon. In a home left empty for the summer, it can mean water running for days. Replacing aging supply lines with new braided stainless ones is inexpensive and one of the best protections against a major loss.

Check your water pressure

High water pressure speeds up wear on every part on this list. Under the International Plumbing Code (IPC), static pressure should not exceed 80 psi. A simple gauge that threads onto a hose bib tells you where your home stands. If the reading is high, a failed pressure reducing valve may explain why several parts seem to be failing at once.

Plan replacements in one visit

It is tempting to fix things one at a time as they fail. In a home where most parts are the same age, it often makes more sense to replace a group together, such as every angle stop and supply line in the kitchen and baths, in a single visit. The cost per part drops and you avoid a string of separate calls.

Leaving for the summer

Many Sun City Grand owners spend summers elsewhere. Before leaving, close the main, put the water heater on vacation or pilot, and arrange for someone to walk through the house every so often, especially after monsoon storms. A house with the water off cannot flood from a burst supply line.

Accessibility upgrades while you are at it

A plumbing refresh is a natural time to think about comfort and safety. Comfort-height toilets, handheld showerheads, and converting a seldom-used tub into a low-threshold shower are common projects in active adult communities. Our shower and tub installation page covers conversions, and general plumbing repair covers valve and supply line replacement.

What is usually not due yet

Not everything in a 20 to 30 year old home needs attention. Drain lines made of plastic generally hold up well for much longer, and the main supply piping in most Sun City Grand homes is not near the end of its life just because of age. The work that makes sense now is on the small moving and sealing parts, plus the water heater.

That distinction matters when someone suggests a large project. A home with no history of pinhole leaks or slab leaks rarely needs a repipe. Replacing valves, supply lines, and the water heater usually delivers most of the protection for a fraction of the cost.

Keep a simple record

Write down what you replace and when, including the water heater's install date and the last time valves or hoses were changed. A single page taped inside the garage cabinet is enough. It makes the next decision easier, and it is useful information if you ever sell.

The same aging-parts pattern shows up a few miles north, where Sun City West homes are older still, built between 1978 and 1997.

What to do first: a simple plan

  1. Find the build year of your home and the age of your water heater.
  2. Check water pressure with a hose bib gauge.
  3. Replace rubber washing machine hoses with braided stainless.
  4. Replace original or unknown-age supply lines at every toilet and faucet.
  5. Exercise every shutoff valve, and replace any that are stiff or drip.
  6. Plan the water heater replacement if it is past 10 years.

None of this is urgent in the sense of an emergency. It is the kind of work that keeps an emergency from happening. For a community built over one decade, the parts are aging together, and dealing with them together is usually the smartest approach.
