Water filtration chosen from what is in your water

Water filtration installation is fitting a system that removes or reduces specific things from your water, such as chlorine taste, sediment, or dissolved solids. The right system depends on what you want gone, which is why the process starts with your supplier's water quality report and a test at the tap.

In Surprise, both EPCOR and the city put out yearly reports for their systems. Those reports show what the utility found and treated for, which narrows the choices before anyone suggests equipment.

Filtration and softening are different jobs. A softener removes hardness minerals. A filter handles taste, odor, and a range of other contaminants. Many homes end up with both, each doing its own part.

Private wells become more common as you leave the city toward the White Tank Mountains and the rural edge of Maricopa County. Inside Surprise, near Bell Road or Loop 303, homes are on utility water, which changes which filter makes sense.

Types of filtration systems

Each type solves a different problem. Buying the wrong one wastes money and space.

Under-sink reverse osmosis

A reverse osmosis system pushes water through a very fine membrane and removes a large share of dissolved solids. It feeds a separate faucet at the kitchen sink for drinking and cooking.

It sends some water to the drain as part of the process, and the membrane and pre-filters need replacing on a schedule.

Best for

Drinking and cooking water

Certified under NSF/ANSI 58 for reverse osmosis systems.

Whole-home carbon filtration

A carbon filter at the main line treats every tap in the house for chlorine taste and odor. It improves shower and laundry water as well as drinking water.

Carbon does not remove hardness, so it pairs with a softener in hard-water homes.

Best for

Chlorine taste and smell everywhere

Look for NSF/ANSI 42 for aesthetic claims like taste and odor.

Sediment and well-water systems

Private wells can carry sand, grit, and other particles that clog fixtures. A sediment filter catches them before they reach the plumbing.

Well owners often need a combination of sediment filtration and other treatment chosen from a lab test.

Best for

Rural homes on private wells

Well water is the owner's to test, since utility reports do not cover it.

Private wells are your responsibility to test

Rural properties west and north of Surprise sometimes draw from private wells. The EPA's public drinking water standards apply to utilities, not private wells, so testing is up to the owner.

A certified lab test is the right first step. It tells you what the well actually has, so the treatment system answers a real problem rather than a guess.

What to do before you buy a filter

These steps keep you from paying for treatment you do not need.

  • 1Read your water quality report. Your supplier's annual report lists what was found in the source and treated water.
  • 2Test at your tap. Plumbing inside the home can change water quality, so a tap test fills in the gaps.
  • 3Name the problem. Taste, odor, spots, or a specific contaminant each points to a different system.
  • 4Check certifications. NSF/ANSI 42, 53, and 58 cover taste and odor, health-related contaminants, and reverse osmosis.
  • 5Plan for upkeep. Every filter needs cartridge or membrane changes. Ask about the schedule and cost before installing.
