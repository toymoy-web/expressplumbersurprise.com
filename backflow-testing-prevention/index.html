Backflow prevention protects the public water supply

Backflow is water flowing the wrong way, from a home or business back into the public water supply. A backflow prevention assembly is a valve arrangement that stops that from happening, so irrigation water, chemicals, or other contaminants cannot be pulled into drinking water.

Backflow can happen when pressure drops in the public main, such as during a main break or heavy firefighting use. Without a working device, water sitting in an irrigation line or a commercial system could be siphoned back.

Under the IPC, any connection that could let contaminants into potable water must have backflow protection. Testable assemblies must also be checked on a schedule to make sure they still work.

What to do when a backflow test is due

A little prep keeps the test quick.

  • 1Find the device. It is usually near the meter, along the irrigation line, or at the building's service entry.
  • 2Clear access. Trim plants and move items away from the assembly.
  • 3Keep the notice. Your provider's notice lists the device and the deadline.
  • 4Plan a short shutoff. Water to the protected line is off briefly during the test.
  • 5Keep the report. The tester's report is filed with the provider. Keep a copy for your records.

If your device fails the test

A failed test usually means worn rubber parts, debris in a check valve, or a relief valve that will not seal. Most failures are repaired with a rebuild kit, then the device is retested.

Hard water and sediment are common culprits in local devices. When a device keeps failing, replacing it with a new assembly may cost less than repeated rebuilds.

Irrigation systems and backflow in Surprise yards

Most Surprise yards are watered by drip or sprinkler systems fed from the house supply. That makes the irrigation connection the most common place a home needs a testable backflow device.

Drip lines sit in soil and mulch, and sprinkler heads sit at ground level where fertilizer and standing water collect. If pressure drops in the street main, that water could be drawn backward without a working device in the line.

Irrigation devices also take a beating from sun and heat. Plastic bonnets crack and rubber seals harden, which is why a device that passed a few years ago can fail today. Replacing a cracked bonnet or a hardened seal is usually a quick fix once it is found.

Backflow test notices come from your water provider, EPCOR or the City of Surprise, not from Maricopa County. Irrigation-heavy neighborhoods such as Sun City Grand and Marley Park have many testable devices on their landscape lines.
